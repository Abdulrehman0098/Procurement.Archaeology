Frontex, the Entry/Exit System, and the Price of a Border Database

The EU's Entry/Exit System is a large-scale IT programme with a border-control purpose. Its procurement record is a case study in schedule risk, agency coordination, and the difficulty of buying a system that many institutions will operate.

One system, many operators

The Entry/Exit System is designed to record the entry and exit of third-country nationals at Schengen borders. It is not a single government's internal database. It is a shared system with a central technical component, national connections, border-checkpoint equipment, and a legal framework that defines who may access what. The procurement challenge follows from that structure.

eu-LISA is the EU agency responsible for the central system. Frontex supports operational cooperation and border management. Member states are responsible for their own border infrastructure and for connecting to the central system. A delay in one layer can delay the whole. A defect in one national implementation can affect confidence in the programme even if the central system works.

This is a common pattern in EU large-scale IT. The contract that builds the central system is only one part of the evidence. The public also needs the national plans, the testing regime, the interface specifications, and the operational readiness assessments. Without those, a central go-live date is a claim about one layer, not the system.

How the procurement is split

EU agencies buy IT through procurement procedures governed by the EU financial regulation. Contracts may cover development, hosting, maintenance, security, and support. In practice, large programmes often use framework contracts and multiple call-offs. That can provide flexibility, but it can also fragment the record across lots, amendments, and service orders.

The supplier landscape is specialised. Large integrators may lead consortiums that include biometrics, hardware, networking, and software firms. A prime contractor may be accountable for integration while much of the technical work is performed by subcontractors. The public record should identify the prime, the material subcontractors, and the allocation of responsibility.

The split between central and national procurement is another visibility problem. A central contract may be published in the EU's procurement journal, while a national border-equipment contract is published on a national portal. Comparing them requires matching identifiers, dates, and descriptions across systems. That is not impossible, but it is not automatic.

Why the schedule slipped

Large IT programmes slip for familiar reasons: requirements change, integration is harder than expected, testing reveals defects, and operational readiness depends on actors outside the supplier's control. The Entry/Exit System has faced repeated delays, and the public explanation has often focused on national readiness and technical testing.

A schedule delay is not automatically a procurement failure. It can be the correct response to a safety or data-protection problem. The analytical question is whether the delay was anticipated, priced, and governed. Was there a realistic test schedule? Were contingency and transition costs included? Did the contract allow the buyer to direct changes without losing control of the baseline?

Delay also changes the value-for-money calculation. A system that arrives later may cost more to maintain, may require temporary workarounds, and may face a different threat environment. Those costs are part of the programme, even if they sit outside the original contract.

Biometrics, fundamental rights, and procurement leverage

Border IT is not a neutral logistics system. It processes personal data, including biometric data, and it affects people's ability to travel. Data protection, proportionality, and access rules are part of the requirement, not an afterthought. A supplier that builds a technically compliant system can still produce an operationally disproportionate one if the buyer has not specified the safeguards.

Procurement is a lever for those safeguards. Contracts can require data-protection impact assessments, security testing, audit rights, retention rules, and documented access controls. They can also require the supplier to explain how the system will be tested against fundamental-rights requirements. If those obligations are not in the contract, they are harder to enforce later.

The oversight challenge is that the relevant expertise is distributed. The data-protection authority understands privacy, the border agency understands operations, the IT agency understands integration, and the auditor understands value for money. A programme that does not connect those perspectives will produce a record that is strong in one dimension and weak in others.

Contract design can help by requiring a single evidence pack for each release: the test results, the data-protection assessment, the security accreditation, and the operational-readiness sign-off. When those documents are assembled together, a reviewer can see whether the system is being accepted as a whole. When they are scattered across agencies, each sign-off can be technically true while the system remains unready.

What the public record can and cannot show

The public record can show the legal basis, the central contracts, the schedule, the audit findings, and the official statements about readiness. It can show whether a contract was awarded competitively, whether it was amended, and what the scope covered. It can also show whether the programme published a testing strategy or a contingency plan.

It is less good at showing the internal trade-offs. A redacted contract may hide the price of a change. A security review may be classified. A national readiness assessment may be summarized rather than published. Those gaps are not necessarily improper, but they limit what an outsider can conclude.

The disciplined response is to state the limit. A reader should be able to tell the difference between a documented delay, an official explanation, and an inference. The same applies to cost. A central contract value is not the whole programme cost, and a national implementation cost is not necessarily comparable across countries.

Another useful check is the audit trail for a single border crossing. Which central system does it query, which national system does it update, which contractor maintains the equipment, and which authority is accountable when a record is wrong? If the answer requires four different documents that do not share an identifier, the governance gap is already visible.

What this case teaches about shared IT

First, map the layers before comparing numbers. A central contract, a national contract, and an operational cost are different objects. Second, treat testing and national readiness as part of the procurement baseline, not as downstream details. Third, require a subcontracting map for any system that handles sensitive data. Fourth, build fundamental-rights and data-protection evidence into the contract, not into a later review.

Fifth, publish a schedule that distinguishes central delivery from operational readiness. A go-live date that depends on 27 national implementations is not a single project milestone. It is a coordination outcome. The public should be able to see which layer is ready and which is not.

The final lesson is about trust. A border database is only as credible as its evidence trail. If the public cannot see how the system was specified, tested, and governed, every delay becomes a reason to doubt the programme. Transparency does not guarantee success, but it makes failure easier to diagnose and correct.

That is why the most valuable documents are often the least dramatic: the interface specification, the test plan, the change log, and the readiness checklist. They do not produce a headline, but they show whether the programme is being managed as a system or merely announced as one.

A parliament or board can use the same test. Ask for the last three change requests, the last three test failures, and the last three national readiness exceptions. If the answer is a narrative rather than a document set, the oversight is not yet operating at the level where problems are visible.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. A shared EU IT system is a stack of central, national, and operational procurements, not one contract.
  2. Schedule slippage should be assessed against the testing baseline and national readiness, not just the go-live date.
  3. Biometric systems need data-protection and fundamental-rights requirements written into the contract.
  4. Subcontracting maps matter when a prime integrator handles sensitive data and critical infrastructure.
  5. A public record that separates documented facts from official explanations is more useful than a single verdict.
