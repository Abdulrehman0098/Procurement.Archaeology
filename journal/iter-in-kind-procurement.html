ITER and the Cost of Learning by Building

ITER is a scientific experiment, an industrial programme, and a diplomatic arrangement. Its in-kind procurement model spreads risk across countries, but it also makes cost and schedule unusually hard to see.

A project that is also an experiment

ITER is designed to test whether fusion can be produced at a scale relevant to a power plant. That makes it different from a conventional infrastructure project. The design has to advance the state of knowledge, and some of the manufacturing challenge is itself an experiment. A first-of-a-kind project cannot be priced with the same confidence as a repeated build.

The procurement model reflects that uncertainty. Instead of one buyer placing all contracts, member countries contribute components through their own domestic agencies. The ITER Organization coordinates the design and assembly, while the members manufacture and deliver parts. The intention is to share cost and risk. The effect is a supply chain with many interfaces and many accountability lines.

For a reader, the first distinction is between the project cost and the member contributions. A member's spending is not necessarily the same as the project's cost, because contributions can be in kind, in staff, or in cash, and because accounting rules differ. Comparing headline numbers across countries is risky without a common definition.

In-kind procurement: benefits and blind spots

In-kind procurement can be sensible for a treaty project. It gives each member an industrial role, builds domestic capability, and avoids asking one country to fund the entire build. It also aligns political commitment with technical contribution. Those are real benefits.

The blind spot is visibility. If a component is late, the delay may sit with a national agency, a supplier, a design change, or an interface problem. The central project schedule can show the effect, but not always the cause. The public record is spread across national audit offices, agency reports, and project documents, each using its own categories.

In-kind contributions also complicate change control. A design change agreed at the centre may require a national agency to renegotiate a contract, retool a factory, or repeat a qualification test. The cost of that change may appear in a national budget rather than in the central project cost. A whole-life cost estimate has to capture those distributed effects.

Why cost estimates move

ITER's cost estimates have changed over the life of the project, as have its schedule assumptions. That is not surprising for a first-of-a-kind machine. The more useful question is whether the changes were managed transparently. Was there a baseline? Was the baseline updated with a clear explanation? Were the member contributions renegotiated in public?

Cost growth in a megaproject can come from technical difficulty, schedule extension, currency movements, labour costs, and scope changes. In a treaty project, it can also come from a mismatch between the central schedule and national budget cycles. A member may fund a contribution in a different period than the one assumed by the project.

The distinction between construction cost and operating cost is especially important. A fusion experiment has a long commissioning and operating phase. The cost of running it, maintaining it, and eventually decommissioning it is part of the public investment. A construction headline is not a whole-life figure.

Currency is another hidden variable. Contributions are made in different currencies and converted under different rules. A project cost expressed in one currency can move even if the underlying work does not change. A rigorous comparison states the exchange-rate assumption and the date, or avoids a single-currency total altogether.

The schedule is a coordination problem

A project of this kind is assembled in a sequence. A delay in one component can affect the installation of another, the commissioning plan, and the research programme. The critical path runs through many organisations, not just one contractor. That makes schedule management a governance problem as much as an engineering one.

The central organisation can set milestones, but it cannot always compel a national agency to prioritise a contribution over domestic constraints. The treaty framework provides mechanisms for coordination, but it does not eliminate the friction. A realistic schedule has to include those institutional dependencies.

For outsiders, the useful evidence is the milestone record: what was delivered, what was late, what was redesigned, and what effect that had on the next stage. A single completion date is less informative than a sequence of verified milestones.

Schedule risk also compounds. A late component can force a different installation sequence, which can consume contingency and affect the commissioning of adjacent systems. The public cost of a delay is therefore not just the cost of keeping a team on site. It includes the lost research time and the cost of rework created by the new sequence.

What auditors can compare

National audit offices and the European Court of Auditors can examine their own country's or institution's contributions. They can ask whether a contract was competed, whether costs were controlled, and whether the contribution was delivered. They cannot easily audit the whole international project, because the treaty structure distributes responsibility.

That makes the central project's own reporting essential. It should provide a common cost framework, a common schedule, and a clear statement of what is included in each member's contribution. Without that, each audit is a partial view.

The public can still learn from the partial views. A national report may reveal a contract-management problem that has implications for the whole project. A central report may reveal an interface risk that national auditors cannot see. Reading them together is more useful than reading either alone.

The key is a common reference. If each report uses a different component name, a different cost category, and a different schedule baseline, the reader cannot assemble the picture. A central register of contributions, with a stable identifier for each component and a dated milestone history, would make the distributed model auditable without changing the treaty structure.

What ITER teaches about first-of-a-kind procurement

First, separate the scientific uncertainty from the procurement uncertainty. A first-of-a-kind project needs a design maturity assessment before a fixed price is credible. Second, define a common cost framework across contributors. Third, treat interfaces as contracts, with clear responsibility and change control. Fourth, publish a milestone record, not just a completion date.

Fifth, budget for commissioning and operations from the start. A machine that is built but cannot be run is not a completed asset. Sixth, accept that in-kind contributions require a stronger central reporting system, not a weaker one, because the risk is distributed.

The final lesson is about patience and evidence. A project that is genuinely advancing knowledge may justify a longer schedule and a higher cost. But the justification has to be documented. Without a clear baseline and a clear change record, the public cannot distinguish learning from drift.

A useful test is whether the project can explain what it has learned in procurement terms. Did a prototype reduce the cost of the next unit? Did a supplier qualification shorten a later schedule? Did a design change remove a known risk? If the answer is only that the project is complex, the learning claim is not yet evidence.

The same test applies to the public record. A lessons-learned report that lists generic recommendations is weaker than a report that names the component, the contract, the change, and the cost. Specificity is what allows the next project to avoid the same mistake rather than merely admire the lesson.

ITER is likely to remain a test of that principle. The project can justify its cost if it produces both scientific knowledge and transferable procurement knowledge. The second part will be judged by whether future fusion projects can point to a documented improvement, not by whether the first machine eventually works.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. In-kind contributions share risk but also distribute the evidence, making central reporting more important.
  2. A first-of-a-kind project needs design-maturity evidence before a fixed-price comparison is meaningful.
  3. National contributions and project costs are different accounting objects and should not be compared directly.
  4. The critical path runs through institutions as well as suppliers, so schedule risk is a governance risk.
  5. Commissioning, operations, and decommissioning belong in the whole-life cost.
