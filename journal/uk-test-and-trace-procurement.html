Test and Trace: When Speed Outran the Paper Trail

England's test-and-trace programme was built at extraordinary speed. The procurement record shows how urgency can deliver capacity and still leave the public unable to test value for money.

Why Test and Trace matters

Test and Trace was one of the largest peacetime public-health procurement efforts ever attempted in England. It had to build testing capacity, contact tracing, digital systems, laboratories, logistics, and a call-centre operation in a very short period. The urgency was real. The question is not whether urgency was justified. The question is what kind of record was created while the programme was moving.

The National Audit Office's work on the programme is essential because it separates the operational story from the accountability story. A programme can deliver tests and still fail to show that the money was well spent. A programme can also be imperfect in delivery and still produce useful evidence for the future. The procurement analyst has to hold both possibilities at once.

Test and Trace is a test case for emergency procurement because it combines almost every risk factor: a new buyer structure, unfamiliar suppliers, rapid scaling, political pressure, and a supply chain that changed as the epidemic changed. It is exactly the environment in which documentation is most likely to slip.

What the public record shows

The public record shows a programme that used multiple procurement routes at once. Some contracts were awarded through existing frameworks. Some were direct awards. Some were call-offs against agreements that had been set up for other purposes. The mix is not inherently wrong; emergency procurement often requires a portfolio of routes. But the mix makes it harder to compare prices and harder to explain why a particular supplier was chosen.

The audit trail also shows the importance of publication. A contract notice is not the same as a contract. An award notice may give a value, but not the statement of work. A published contract may redact commercial information. The public can see that a contract exists without being able to see enough to test whether the price was reasonable.

Parliamentary scrutiny added questions about targets, staffing, and the use of consultants. Those questions are not all procurement questions, but they intersect with procurement. If a programme is measured by a target, the contracting strategy will follow the target. If the target changes, the contracts may change with it.

The money trail

Emergency spending is often described with a single large number. That number is usually a budget, a forecast, or an announced envelope, not a final audited cost. The distinction matters. A budget can be spent, underspent, or reallocated. A forecast can change. A final cost requires a defined boundary around the programme.

The second issue is the unit of analysis. A testing programme buys laboratory capacity, reagents, logistics, software, call-centre minutes, and management. Comparing the price of one component with the price of another is rarely meaningful. A better question is whether the buyer can show that the chosen mix was the best available option under the constraints of the moment.

The third issue is competition. In an emergency, the pool of available suppliers may be small. That can justify a direct award. But the buyer should still record why the pool was small, what alternatives were considered, and what steps were taken to test the price. Without that record, the public cannot distinguish a justified sole-source award from a convenience award.

The accountability gap

The central accountability gap is between operational reporting and financial evidence. A programme can report how many tests it processed and how many contacts it reached. Those are outputs. They do not by themselves show whether the programme was cost-effective, whether the supplier margins were reasonable, or whether a different design would have produced more public value.

There is also a gap between the centre and the edge of the supply chain. A national programme may contract with a prime supplier that then uses subcontractors for laboratories, transport, or software. The public may see the prime contract and not the subcontracts. If the subcontracting layer is where the work actually happens, the visible contract is only part of the story.

Finally, there is a temporal gap. Emergency records are often created after the decision. A decision log written weeks later is weaker evidence than a contemporaneous note. The longer the gap, the harder it is to reconstruct why a particular route was chosen.

What the audits did not settle

Audit findings about value for money do not automatically mean that a programme was wasteful. They mean that the buyer did not provide enough evidence to demonstrate value. That is a serious finding, but it is a finding about evidence, not necessarily about outcomes. The distinction is essential in public debate.

It is also difficult to know what the counterfactual would have been. A slower procurement might have produced better competition and lower prices, but it might also have delayed capacity during an emergency. The honest answer is that the counterfactual is uncertain. A good analysis says so.

What remains unresolved is the distribution of risk and reward. Did the suppliers bear meaningful risk? Did the public bear the downside while the supplier earned a fee? Were the contracts structured to reward delivery or simply to pay for activity? Those questions can be examined without alleging misconduct.

What changed after the scrutiny

The legacy of Test and Trace is a set of lessons about emergency procurement: document the decision, publish what can be published, record the subcontracting chain, and build a data model that allows later comparison. The test is whether those lessons were embedded in new guidance or simply written into reports.

One useful reform is a standard emergency procurement record. It would capture the buyer, the supplier, the legal route, the competition, the price basis, the deliverables, the subcontractors, and the accountable official. That record should be created at the time of the award, not after an audit.

Another useful reform is a public dashboard that distinguishes budget, commitment, payment, and final cost. The technology is not the hard part. The hard part is agreeing on definitions and maintaining the discipline to update them.

What researchers should take from this

First, identify the procurement route for each contract, not just the programme as a whole. Second, separate outputs from value-for-money evidence. Third, look for the subcontracting layer. Fourth, compare the published record with the audit's description of the record. Fifth, ask what a contemporaneous decision log would have contained.

Test and Trace also shows why procurement research needs a timeline. The programme changed as the epidemic changed. A contract awarded in March may have been justified by a different set of facts from a contract awarded in November. Reading the whole programme as a single decision hides those changes.

The final lesson is about language. "Emergency" is a justification, not an explanation. A serious account should explain what was urgent, what alternatives were available, and what evidence was created to support the choice. That is how a case study becomes useful rather than merely political.

A useful comparison is with other emergency programmes that faced the same constraint. Did they use the same route, the same contract type, and the same level of disclosure? A difference is not proof of wrongdoing, but it is a reason to ask why. A similarity is not proof of good practice, but it is a baseline for the next review.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. Emergency procurement can be legitimate, but it requires a deliberate record rather than an after-the-fact narrative.
  2. Outputs such as tests delivered are not the same as evidence of value for money.
  3. A mix of procurement routes makes comparison harder and raises the value of a clear decision log.
  4. The subcontracting layer is often where the public record becomes thin.
  5. The counterfactual — what a slower procurement would have delivered — is uncertain and should be described as uncertain.
