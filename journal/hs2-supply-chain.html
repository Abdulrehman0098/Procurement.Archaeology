HS2: The Supply Chain Behind the Headline Cost

HS2 is usually discussed as a number. The more useful question is how a megaproject buys, subcontracts, and accounts for risk across a supply chain that stretches far beyond the names on the award notice.

Why HS2 is a procurement story

HS2 is often treated as a transport debate. It is also a procurement case study on an unusual scale. A megaproject buys design, engineering, construction, land, utilities, environmental mitigation, and programme management. It does so over years, through multiple contract packages, with a supply chain that includes thousands of firms.

The headline cost is important, but it is not the whole story. The cost is produced by a set of procurement decisions: what was scoped, how risk was allocated, how contracts were priced, how changes were handled, and how the lower tiers were managed. If those decisions are opaque, the headline number becomes a political symbol rather than an accountable fact.

HS2 is also a case about time. A long project can be re-scoped, paused, or accelerated. Each change can create new procurement obligations and new costs. The public record has to connect the programme's history to the contracts that were actually signed.

What the public record shows

The public record shows a programme with a large number of contracts and a complex delivery structure. Tier-one contractors are visible because they sign the major awards. The lower tiers are less visible, even though much of the work happens there. That visibility gap is a recurring feature of megaprojects.

Audit reports have focused on cost, schedule, and the programme's ability to deliver. Those findings are not just engineering findings. They are findings about the procurement model: whether the contracts gave the buyer enough control, whether the risk transfer was real, and whether the programme had the capability to manage the interfaces between packages.

Parliamentary scrutiny has added questions about land, local impacts, and value for money. Those questions often sit outside the contract itself, but they affect the procurement. A change in scope driven by politics becomes a contract change driven by procurement.

The money trail

Megaproject costs are usually presented as a range or an estimate in a particular price base. That is not a trick; it is a recognition that the final cost is uncertain. The problem is that a range can be used to avoid accountability. A public reader needs to know what is included, what is excluded, and when the estimate was made.

The contract structure matters as much as the estimate. A fixed-price contract can transfer risk to the contractor, but it can also lead to claims if the scope changes. A cost-reimbursable contract can give the buyer more flexibility, but it can also weaken the incentive to control cost. Most megaprojects use a mixture. The public should be able to see which model applies to which package.

The supply chain adds another layer. A tier-one contractor may subcontract a large share of the work. The public sees the tier-one price, not the subcontract prices. That does not mean the tier-one price is wrong. It means the value-for-money analysis is incomplete without some visibility into the lower tiers.

The accountability gap

The accountability gap in HS2 is partly a capability gap. A buyer that manages a complex programme needs commercial, engineering, and data capability. If that capability is outsourced, the buyer may lose the ability to challenge the supplier's assumptions. The contracts may still be legally sound, but the buyer's practical control may be weaker than the documents suggest.

There is also a gap between the programme's stated objectives and the contracts' incentives. If the objective is to deliver a railway, but the contracts reward design progress or construction milestones, the supplier may optimize for the milestone rather than the outcome. That is a classic principal-agent problem, and it is visible in the procurement record only if the incentives are published.

Finally, there is a gap between the national debate and the local reality. A project can be debated as a single national programme while the actual work is delivered through local packages with local subcontractors. The public record should allow a reader to connect the two.

What the audits did not settle

Audit reports can establish that a programme's cost estimate increased or that its schedule slipped. They do not automatically establish that the programme should be cancelled, that a different route would have been cheaper, or that a particular contractor performed poorly. Those are separate judgments.

It is also difficult to compare a megaproject with a counterfactual. A smaller project might have cost less, but it might also have delivered fewer benefits. A different procurement model might have transferred more risk, but it might also have reduced competition or increased the cost of capital. The honest analysis acknowledges those trade-offs.

What remains unresolved is the distribution of risk. When a cost overrun occurs, who bears it? The public, the contractor, the insurer, or the subcontractor? The answer depends on the contract terms and the claims process. That is where the next layer of research should go.

What changed after the scrutiny

Megaprojects often respond to scrutiny with better reporting. Cost and schedule data become more granular, risk registers are published, and parliamentary committees receive more frequent updates. Those reforms are useful, but they are not a substitute for commercial capability.

A more substantive reform is to publish the contract model and the risk allocation for each major package. That would allow the public to see where risk sits and how the buyer intends to manage it. Another is to require a supply-chain map for major packages, at least at the tier-one and tier-two levels.

The test is whether the next phase of the programme can be explained in plain language: what is being bought, from whom, at what price, with what risk, and with what evidence of competition. If the answer is yes, the procurement is more accountable even if the project is still expensive.

What researchers should take from this

First, separate the programme estimate from the contract values. Second, identify the contract model for each package. Third, follow the supply chain beyond the tier-one names. Fourth, connect scope changes to contract changes. Fifth, ask who bears the risk when the estimate moves.

HS2 also shows why procurement research needs engineering literacy. A contract clause about ground conditions or interface risk may look technical, but it can determine who pays for a delay. The analyst does not need to be an engineer, but they need to know which technical assumptions have commercial consequences.

The final lesson is about patience. A megaproject's procurement record is long and uneven. The most important document may be an amendment, a risk report, or a committee note rather than the original award. A serious analysis follows the project over time.

It should also follow the money beyond the headline contract. A programme can report a tier-one award while the most important commercial decisions sit in a package, a variation, or a local subcontract. The public record is a map of those decisions, not a single number at the centre.

That map should include the dates. A cost that moved after a design change is a different fact from a cost that moved after a contractual dispute. The sequence is the explanation.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. A megaproject's headline cost is produced by many procurement decisions, not one.
  2. Tier-one visibility is not the same as supply-chain visibility.
  3. Risk allocation is the key to understanding who pays when a cost estimate moves.
  4. Audit findings about cost and schedule are not automatically findings about contractor misconduct.
  5. Long projects require longitudinal procurement research, not a single snapshot.
