The Dutch Childcare Scandal and the Governance of a Risk Model

The Dutch childcare benefits scandal was not only a data-protection failure. It was a failure to govern a risk-scoring system whose outputs were treated as facts. That is a procurement lesson for every public buyer of algorithmic tools.

What the system was asked to do

The Dutch childcare benefits system was designed to detect fraud and error in a large, complex benefit programme. Administrators needed a way to prioritise cases. A risk-classification model promised to help by ranking cases according to indicators associated with risk. That is a familiar public-sector use of data.

The problem was not that a model existed. It was that the model's outputs were treated as a reason to act without enough individual evidence. A risk score is a probability statement, not a finding of wrongdoing. When an organisation converts a score into a suspicion, it has made a decision that requires its own justification.

The scandal is often described as an algorithm case. It is also a procurement and governance case. The buyer acquired data, systems, and advice, but did not establish who was accountable for the model's use, how its errors would be detected, or how a citizen could challenge a decision.

Data quality is a governance issue

Risk models depend on data. If the underlying data is incomplete, outdated, or recorded for a different purpose, the model can produce misleading results. In a benefits system, a missing document may reflect administrative complexity rather than fraud. A mismatch may reflect a data-entry error rather than a false claim.

Procurement can lock in those problems. A contract may specify a data feed without specifying its quality, provenance, or permitted use. A supplier may deliver a model that performs well on a test set but fails in the real administrative process. The buyer needs the right to audit the data and the model, not just to receive the output.

The governance question is who owns the decision. A model can rank cases, but a human official must decide. If the official has neither the time nor the evidence to challenge the ranking, the model is effectively deciding. That is a design choice, and it should be documented in the procurement requirement.

The legal boundary

The Dutch data-protection authority found that the processing of personal data in the childcare benefits case was unlawful in important respects. The finding concerned the use of data, the legal basis, and the safeguards. It was not a general judgment about all algorithmic tools.

The case shows why legal review must be part of the procurement process, not a sign-off at the end. A system that is technically capable of producing a risk score may still be unlawful if the legal basis is missing or the data is used for an incompatible purpose. A supplier cannot cure that problem with better code.

The European data-protection framework adds requirements for transparency, human intervention, and impact assessment in some cases. Those requirements should appear in the contract as deliverables and audit rights. If they are only in a policy document, they are easier to ignore.

Human oversight needs resources

Human oversight is often described as a safeguard. In practice, it requires time, training, and access to evidence. An official who can only see a risk score cannot provide meaningful oversight. An official who can see the underlying data but has a backlog of cases may still rubber-stamp the score.

The procurement requirement should therefore specify the oversight workflow, not just the model. Who reviews a flagged case? What evidence must they consider? How is a challenge recorded? What happens when the model and the official disagree? Those questions determine whether oversight is real.

The scandal also shows the cost of not funding oversight. A cheaper model with a weak review process can produce far higher social and legal costs than a more expensive system with proper safeguards. The business case should include those downstream costs.

The record a citizen needs

A person affected by an algorithmic decision needs to know that a model was used, what data it relied on, and how to challenge the result. That is not only a privacy right. It is a basic requirement of administrative fairness.

Procurement can support that requirement by demanding an audit trail. The system should record the model version, the input data, the score, the reviewer, and the decision. Without that trail, an appeal becomes an exercise in reconstructing the past. With it, the citizen and the reviewer can test the reasoning.

The trail also helps the buyer. If a model drifts or a data source changes, the buyer can see the effect. A system that only stores the final decision cannot be managed or corrected.

Buying assurance, not just software

An algorithmic system needs assurance: testing for accuracy and bias, a review of the legal basis, a data-quality assessment, and a process for handling complaints. Those services can be bought from a supplier, but the buyer remains accountable for the decision. Outsourcing the assurance does not outsource the responsibility.

A procurement requirement should separate the model vendor from the assurance provider. If the same supplier builds the model and certifies its fairness, the review is less independent. That does not mean the supplier is dishonest. It means the buyer needs a second line of evidence.

Assurance also has to continue after go-live. Models drift, data changes, and new legal guidance appears. A one-off test at delivery is not enough. The contract should include periodic review, incident reporting, and a right to audit the model in operation.

Redress is part of the system design

People affected by a risk score need a route to challenge it. That route should be simple, documented, and independent enough to be credible. A call centre that cannot explain the score is not a redress mechanism. A review that never changes an outcome is not oversight.

Procurement can require the supplier to support redress: to explain the model's inputs, to reproduce a decision, and to provide the records needed for an appeal. Those obligations should survive the end of the contract, so that a citizen can still obtain an explanation years later.

Redress also produces data. The reasons for successful appeals show where the model or the data is failing. A buyer that does not collect and analyse that data is missing a free source of assurance.

A redress mechanism should be tested before launch. Can a reviewer reproduce a decision? Can they see the data? Can they change the outcome? If the answer is no, the mechanism is a communications channel, not a remedy. Testing it with realistic cases is part of acceptance, not an optional pilot.

The test should include the hardest cases, not the easy ones. A model that works for a straightforward file may fail when income is irregular, a document is missing, or a household has changed. Those are exactly the cases where an automated score is most likely to be wrong and where human judgment matters most.

What public buyers should contract for

First, require a legal basis and a data-protection impact assessment before the model goes live. Second, specify data quality, provenance, and permitted use. Third, require an audit trail for every decision. Fourth, resource human review and define its authority. Fifth, test for discriminatory effects and publish the methodology.

Sixth, separate the model from the decision. A score can inform a decision, but it should not be the decision. The contract should make that separation explicit, with a named accountable role for the human reviewer.

The final lesson is about humility. A risk model is a tool for allocating attention, not a truth machine. The Dutch case is a reminder that the most important procurement requirement may be the one that limits how the tool can be used.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. A risk score is a probability statement, not a finding of wrongdoing.
  2. Data quality and permitted use are governance requirements, not technical details.
  3. Legal review must be part of procurement, not a final sign-off.
  4. Human oversight needs time, evidence, and authority to be meaningful.
  5. An audit trail is essential for appeals, correction, and model management.
