Emergency procurement

The EU's Vaccine Contracts and the Cost of Confidentiality

The EU bought COVID-19 vaccines at unprecedented speed through advance purchase agreements. The record shows how urgency, secrecy, and joint buying can deliver doses while leaving the public unable to test the deal.

Buying in an emergency

The EU's vaccine procurement began in a situation that had no good template. Governments needed doses before manufacturers had completed large-scale trials, and they needed to commit money before the probability of success was known. A normal procurement would have waited for a defined product and a competitive market. There was neither.

Advance purchase agreements were the instrument that made the bet possible. A buyer commits to purchase doses if a vaccine is authorised, and the manufacturer uses that commitment to finance manufacturing capacity. The arrangement shares risk, but it also shifts uncertainty onto the public balance sheet. If the vaccine fails, the money may be lost. If it succeeds, the buyer still has to negotiate price, delivery, and liability.

The emergency context explains the speed. It does not explain every choice. The analytical question is whether the buyer documented the trade-offs, protected the public interest, and created a record that could be reviewed after the emergency. Those tests apply even when the timetable is compressed.

Joint buying and the member-state problem

The Commission negotiated on behalf of member states, which gave the EU more leverage than any single country would have had. A joint buyer can secure volume, coordinate distribution, and avoid a bidding war among member states. That is a genuine public-good rationale for centralised procurement.

Joint buying also creates a principal-agent problem. The Commission negotiates, but member states bear the cost and the political consequences. Their preferences may differ: one country may want a particular manufacturer, another may prioritise speed over price, and a third may care most about liability. A joint mandate has to reconcile those preferences before the negotiation starts.

The distribution mechanism adds another layer. Doses are allocated according to a population-based key, but a country may receive a product it did not approve for its own population or may need to redirect doses because of storage requirements. Those operational choices are part of the procurement outcome, not a separate logistics story.

Price, confidentiality, and the evidentiary gap

Vaccine contracts were initially treated as commercially confidential. Redacted versions were later published, but key terms remained hidden. The stated reason was that disclosure could undermine the manufacturer's negotiating position and reveal sensitive manufacturing information. That rationale is not frivolous.

The accountability problem is that confidentiality also hides the basis for the price. The public could see that doses were bought, but not always the unit price, the payment schedule, the liability arrangement, or the penalties for late delivery. Without those terms, a value-for-money judgment is difficult. A auditor can review the contract, but the public cannot test the auditor's reasoning against the same evidence.

The EU institutions responded with published redacted contracts and audit reports. That was an improvement, but it did not fully close the gap. A redacted contract can still show the structure of the deal, and a good audit can explain the trade-offs. The lesson is to publish the structure early, even when the numbers must remain confidential.

Liability and the limits of a standard clause

Vaccine contracts involved indemnification and liability arrangements that were unusual for public procurement. Manufacturers sought protection against adverse events, and governments accepted a share of the risk in exchange for speed. The exact terms varied by contract and were not fully public.

From a procurement perspective, the issue is not whether indemnity is good or bad in the abstract. It is whether the buyer understood the risk it was taking and priced it. A liability clause can be a rational response to an emergency, but it can also transfer a large contingent liability to the public without a clear ceiling. The record should show the scope, the cap, and the rationale.

Liability also interacts with compensation schemes. If a public scheme covers vaccine injury, the contractual indemnity may be less important to an individual claimant but still important to the public purse. A complete analysis has to connect the contract to the compensation system, not treat them as separate worlds.

Delivery promises and manufacturing reality

Vaccine manufacturing is a fragile, multi-stage process. A contract can promise a delivery schedule, but the schedule depends on suppliers, fill-finish capacity, quality control, and regulatory approvals. When a manufacturer missed a target, the public explanation often pointed to a production issue rather than a contractual failure.

The procurement question is how the contract allocated that risk. Did it include delivery penalties, step-in rights, technology transfer obligations, or alternative sourcing? Did the buyer have visibility into the manufacturer's capacity plan? Did it require diversification across manufacturers and sites? Those terms determine whether a delay is a shared problem or a one-sided public loss.

The EU's portfolio approach was designed to diversify risk by buying from several manufacturers and platforms. That was prudent. A portfolio does not eliminate delay, but it reduces the chance that one failure leaves the whole population unprotected. The value of diversification is part of the procurement case, not an afterthought.

What a reader should compare

A reader should compare the contracts on structure, not only on headline price. Does the agreement commit the buyer to a fixed volume or an option? Is the price per dose fixed or indexed? Who bears the cost of storage, distribution, and wastage? What are the delivery windows, and what happens if they slip? Those terms determine the real cost of the deal.

The portfolio also needs to be read as a portfolio. A lower price from one manufacturer may be less valuable if that manufacturer has a single production site and a higher risk of disruption. A higher price from another may buy diversification that protects the whole programme. The comparison should reflect the risk each contract removes, not just the price it quotes.

Finally, compare the published record with the audit record. If the audit describes a risk that the published contract does not show, that gap is part of the story. The reader should not have to choose between trusting the contract and trusting the auditor. A good publication strategy lets the two be read together.

One more comparison is worth making: the cost of the doses against the cost of the delivery system. A cheap dose that requires expensive ultra-cold storage, new distribution capacity, and a large communications campaign may be more expensive than a costlier dose that fits the existing system. The procurement decision has to include the last mile, not stop at the factory gate.

A final test is whether the buyer can explain the difference between a dose secured and a dose administered. The first is a contract milestone. The second is a public-health outcome. A programme that reports only the first has not yet shown that the procurement achieved its purpose.

What should be published after an emergency

First, publish the contract structure even when the price is redacted: the parties, the product, the volume, the delivery window, the liability model, and the termination rights. Second, explain the negotiation mandate and the trade-offs the buyer accepted. Third, publish an audit that tests the process, not only the outcome. Fourth, separate the purchase decision from the distribution decision.

Fifth, preserve the records that explain why a particular manufacturer was chosen. In an emergency, the choice may be justified by manufacturing capacity or platform diversity rather than by price. That rationale should be written down at the time, not reconstructed later.

The final lesson is about trust. A confidential contract can be legitimate, but a public buyer must find a way to demonstrate that confidentiality did not become a shield. The test is whether an independent reviewer can see the trade-offs and explain them to the public. If not, the emergency may be over but the accountability deficit remains.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. Advance purchase agreements share risk but can leave the public holding a large contingent liability.
  2. Joint EU buying increases leverage but requires member states to reconcile different preferences.
  3. Publishing a redacted contract structure is better than publishing nothing, even when prices stay confidential.
  4. Delivery risk should be allocated through penalties, diversification, and visibility into manufacturing capacity.
  5. An emergency procurement still needs a contemporaneous record of why a particular deal was chosen.
