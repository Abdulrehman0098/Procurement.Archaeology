ArriveCAN and the Contracting Black Box

A pandemic border app became a case study in what happens when urgency, outsourcing, and weak record-keeping meet. The most important finding is not the invoice. It is the missing paper trail.

Why this app became a procurement test case

ArriveCAN began as a practical problem: a government needed a digital way to collect border and public-health information during a pandemic. It ended as a procurement case study because the question changed. The question was no longer whether the app worked. It was whether the government could explain how it was bought, who did the work, what each layer cost, and whether the public received value for money.

That shift matters. Emergency technology procurement is not unusual. Governments often need to move faster than normal rules allow, and they often rely on contractors because the public service does not have enough specialist capacity. The risk is not speed by itself. The risk is speed without a record. When the paper trail is thin, the public cannot tell whether a premium was justified, whether a subcontractor was necessary, or whether a cheaper option was considered.

ArriveCAN is useful precisely because it is not a simple story about one bad invoice. It is a story about a system: urgent need, a compressed timetable, a multi-layer supplier chain, and an accountability mechanism that arrived after the money had been spent.

What the public record shows

The Office of the Auditor General's examination is the anchor for any serious reading of the case. The audit examined the application's development and contracting, and it found weaknesses in how the government demonstrated value for money. It also found that documentation was inadequate in important respects. Those are procurement findings, not just IT findings.

The public record also shows a gap between the simplicity of the user experience and the complexity of the supply chain. A traveller saw a form. A procurement system saw a buyer, a set of contracts, a group of firms, and a chain of responsibility that was difficult to reconstruct after the fact. The more layers there are, the more important it becomes to identify the prime contractor, the subcontractors, the work each performed, and the price attached to each layer.

Parliamentary committees and the media added another layer of scrutiny. Their work is not a substitute for the audit, but it shows how a procurement case becomes a public accountability case. Questions about contractor roles, rates, and selection turned a technical project into a debate about how government buys digital services.

The money trail

The headline number is easy to repeat and hard to interpret. A reported total cost can include development, hosting, support, communications, administration, and work by several departments. It may or may not include internal staff time. It may be an estimate, an expenditure, or a combination of both. The first discipline is therefore to ask what the number measures.

The second discipline is to separate the prime contract from the subcontracting layer. A prime contractor may coordinate work, carry risk, and provide a single point of accountability. That can be valuable. But if a large share of the work is subcontracted, the public still needs to know who did what and how the price was built. A mark-up on a subcontract is not automatically improper, but it is a fact that should be visible.

The third discipline is to compare the procurement route with the alternatives. Was there a competitive process? Was it a directed award? Was a framework used? Were there amendments? The audit's concern about value for money is ultimately a concern about whether the buyer could show that the chosen route was reasonable, not merely whether the final product existed.

The accountability gap

The most important accountability gap in ArriveCAN is not a single missing signature. It is the gap between the government's ability to describe the project and its ability to prove the project's cost and value. A government can always produce a narrative after the fact. The harder test is whether the narrative is supported by contemporaneous records: who approved the work, what options were considered, what the contractor was asked to do, and how the price was tested.

This is where emergency procurement becomes risky. Normal controls are often designed to slow a project down in exchange for evidence. When those controls are relaxed, the evidence has to be created deliberately. If it is not, the project may still deliver a working product, but it will fail the accountability test. That failure can cost more than the original contract because it consumes public trust and parliamentary time.

The case also shows why "the app worked" is not a complete defence. Delivery is one dimension of value. Cost, competition, documentation, and stewardship are others. A procurement system that can only measure delivery will keep producing cases like this.

What the audit did not settle

An audit can establish that documentation was weak or that value for money was not demonstrated. It does not automatically establish that every dollar was wasted, that every contractor acted improperly, or that a different procurement route would have produced a better outcome. Those are separate questions, and they require separate evidence.

It is also important to distinguish between a finding and an allegation. A finding is a conclusion supported by the auditor's evidence. An allegation is a claim that still needs proof. Public debate often blurs the two, especially when a case becomes politically charged. A careful reader should keep the audit's precise language in view and resist the temptation to turn "not demonstrated" into "proven false".

The unresolved questions are still useful. Did the government consider a simpler or cheaper option? How much of the work was truly urgent? What did each layer of contractor add? What would a well-documented emergency procurement have looked like? Those questions can be asked without asserting misconduct.

What changed after the scrutiny

Cases like ArriveCAN usually produce procedural reform: new guidance, new review thresholds, new reporting requirements, or new central oversight. The details matter less than the test. Does the reform make the next emergency contract easier to reconstruct? Does it require a named accountable official, a documented options analysis, and a visible subcontracting chain? Does it create a record that an auditor can follow without interviewing everyone who was in the room?

Reform can also go wrong. If the response is simply more paperwork, it may slow good projects without improving evidence. The better response is targeted: clearer decision logs, standard data fields, and a requirement that the buyer can explain the price and the supply chain in plain language.

For outside researchers, the lesson is to watch implementation, not just announcements. A new policy is a claim. The evidence is whether the next urgent procurement produces a record that can be audited.

What researchers should take from this

First, treat the audit as a map, not a verdict. Read the specific findings and the evidence behind them. Second, separate the total cost from the contract value and the amount paid. Third, follow the subcontracting chain. Fourth, compare the emergency route with the normal route and ask what evidence was created to justify the difference. Fifth, distinguish a documentation failure from a delivery failure; they are not the same.

ArriveCAN is also a reminder that digital procurement is procurement. The fact that the product is an app does not make the contracting rules disappear. Software has hosting, licensing, support, security, and data-protection costs that can continue long after launch. A proper value-for-money analysis has to look at the whole life of the service, not just the build.

The final lesson is about public trust. A government can survive a costly app. It has a harder time surviving the perception that it cannot explain what it bought. The record is the defence. When the record is missing, the argument is lost before it starts.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. Urgency can justify a faster route, but it does not remove the need for a contemporaneous record.
  2. A total cost figure is not the same as a contract value, a payment, or a whole-life cost.
  3. Subcontracting layers must be visible if the public is to understand where the money went.
  4. An audit finding of weak value-for-money evidence is serious, but it is not the same as proof of waste or misconduct.
  5. Digital procurement should be assessed over the whole life of the service, including hosting, support, and security.
