Galileo and the Price of Sovereignty

Galileo is both a satellite navigation system and an industrial policy. Its procurement record shows how security, competition, and geographic return can pull a programme in different directions at once.

Why Galileo is a procurement problem

Galileo is often described as Europe's answer to other global navigation systems. That framing is political, but it has procurement consequences. The programme is not just buying satellites. It is buying a guaranteed service, a security architecture, a ground segment, and a long-term industrial capability. Each of those objectives can imply a different supplier, contract structure, and price.

A purely commercial buyer would ask which supplier can deliver the service at the lowest whole-life cost. A sovereign buyer also asks who controls the technology, where the intellectual property sits, and what happens if a foreign supplier is denied export licences. Those questions can justify a higher price, but they must be stated as objectives rather than hidden inside a technical requirement.

The first analytical step is therefore to separate the public-interest rationale from the delivery record. Sovereignty can be a legitimate reason to pay a premium. It is not a reason to skip the evidence on cost, schedule, and competition.

The procurement model is part of the product

Galileo has been delivered through a mix of EU and ESA procurement, with different rules and different reporting lines. ESA brings technical expertise and a geographic-return principle, under which work is distributed among member states in proportion to their contributions. The EU brings the programme budget and the policy framework.

Geographic return is a legitimate industrial-policy instrument. It can also conflict with a simple best-value competition. If a work package must go to a particular country to satisfy a return rule, the buyer may not get the cheapest or most capable supplier for that package. The trade-off can be defended, but it should be visible.

The contract structure also matters. A large system integrator may be responsible for assembling contributions from many subcontractors. That can create a clear point of accountability, but it can also add a layer of cost and obscure the performance of the underlying suppliers. The public record should show the prime, the major subcontracts, and the responsibility split.

Development, deployment, and operations are different bills

Space programmes have long lives. The development phase builds the technology. The deployment phase launches and commissions the system. The operations phase runs it. A later phase replaces satellites and upgrades the ground segment. A headline cost that covers only one phase is not a whole-life cost.

Galileo's funding has come through successive EU budget periods, which means the programme has had to renew its financial basis several times. That is normal for a long infrastructure programme, but it complicates comparison. A cost overrun in one phase may be a rephasing rather than new money, and a new budget line may include work that was previously funded elsewhere.

The security dimension adds cost that is hard to benchmark. A navigation system has to protect signals, control centres, and ground stations. Security accreditation can drive design choices, supplier vetting, and redundancy. Those costs are real, but they are not easily compared with a commercial navigation service that does not carry the same sovereign obligations.

A useful discipline is to keep a cost breakdown by purpose: build the constellation, operate the ground segment, maintain security, replace satellites, and fund the industrial base. When those purposes are merged into one number, the public cannot see which objective is driving the cost. When they are separated, a debate about sovereignty becomes a debate about a specific trade-off.

Industrial policy in a small supplier market

The space manufacturing market is concentrated. A small number of large firms can lead complex satellite and ground-segment work, and a wider group of specialised firms supplies components and services. That concentration limits the number of credible bidders for a large prime contract.

Competition can still be meaningful at the lot level. A programme can split work into packages that smaller firms can bid for, or require a prime to compete subcontracts. Those choices affect both value for money and the industrial base. If every package is bundled into one mega-contract, the buyer may get integration convenience but lose the discipline of multiple bidders.

There is also a tension between European strategic autonomy and global supply chains. A European prime may rely on non-European components. A security requirement may demand a European source, which can raise costs. The public record should show where the critical dependencies are, not just where the prime contractor is registered.

Lot design is the practical lever. If a programme wants both a strong prime and a competitive supplier base, it can require the prime to compete defined packages, publish subcontract opportunities, and justify any sole-source choice. Those conditions do not guarantee competition, but they create a record that an auditor can test. Without them, industrial return becomes a distribution exercise rather than a capability strategy.

What audit institutions can and cannot settle

The European Court of Auditors has examined EU space programmes and highlighted governance, cost, and schedule risks. Those findings are useful because they focus on the programme's own evidence rather than on political claims. But an audit cannot decide whether sovereignty is worth the price. That is a policy judgment.

An audit can ask whether the buyer defined the requirement, whether the competition was effective, whether the contract managed risk, and whether the reported cost is complete. It can also ask whether the programme's governance arrangements created unclear accountability. Those are the questions that matter for procurement.

The limit is that much of the security and commercial detail is not public. A researcher may see the contract award but not the security annex. That does not make the record useless. It means the analysis should distinguish between what is documented, what is inferred, and what is out of reach.

The procurement record can still answer basic questions. Was the award made by competitive procedure? How many bids were received? Was the contract amended, and for what? Did the buyer publish a technical specification that explains the security requirements? Those facts are often enough to identify where the accountability risk sits, even when the annex remains closed.

How to read a strategic space programme

First, state the policy objectives and the procurement objectives separately. Second, map the funding phases and do not compare a development cost with a whole-life cost. Third, identify the competition at each lot, not just at the prime level. Fourth, trace critical dependencies through the subcontracting chain. Fifth, treat security accreditation as a cost driver with a schedule.

Galileo also shows why governance design is not administrative detail. When two agencies with different rules and different member-state incentives are involved, the handoffs can become the programme's biggest risk. A clear decision rights matrix is part of the procurement architecture.

The final lesson is about honesty in strategic programmes. Sovereignty may justify a premium, but the premium should be stated. If the public is told only that the programme is strategic, it cannot judge whether the delivery model is efficient. If it is told the cost and the trade-offs, it can make a more informed judgment.

That does not require publishing every security secret. It requires publishing the structure of the deal: who bears the risk, who owns the intellectual property, how long the service must last, and what happens if a supplier fails. Those are the terms that turn a strategic ambition into an accountable procurement.

A reader should also ask what is being bought in the operations phase. A satellite system is a service, not only a constellation. The contract for operating, maintaining, and upgrading it may matter more over time than the original construction award. If that contract is not visible, the public record covers the beginning of the story but not the part that determines the long-run price.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. Sovereignty can justify a premium, but the premium should be separated from the delivery cost.
  2. Development, deployment, operations, and replacement are distinct budget problems.
  3. Geographic-return rules can conflict with best-value competition and should be made visible.
  4. Security accreditation is a cost and schedule driver, not a paperwork step.
  5. A concentrated supplier market makes lot design and subcontract competition especially important.
