Framework Agreements and the Subcontracting Blind Spot

Frameworks can shorten procurement and widen access. They can also hide the moment when a public contract becomes a private supply chain. The fix is not to abolish frameworks, but to publish the call-offs and the subcontracts.

What a framework is for

A framework agreement sets the terms under which a buyer can place orders over a period. It is useful when the buyer knows it will need a category of work but cannot specify every requirement in advance. Instead of running a full competition for each order, the buyer can call off from a pre-competed set of suppliers.

The model can reduce transaction costs, shorten lead times, and give suppliers a predictable route to public work. It can also widen access for smaller firms if the framework is divided into lots and the call-offs are open to competition. Those are real benefits, not just procurement convenience.

The risk is that the framework becomes the whole story. The public sees the vehicle and the admitted suppliers, but not the orders, the prices, the subcontracts, or the work actually performed. A framework can be transparent at the top and opaque at the bottom.

LayerWhat the public may see
FrameworkThe vehicle, its scope, and the admitted suppliers.
Call-offThe specific order, sometimes published, sometimes not.

SubcontractOften invisible or only summarized in a plan.

The call-off is the real contract

A framework is a permission to buy, not a purchase. The call-off is where the buyer commits money and scope. If the call-off is not published, the public cannot see what was bought, from whom, at what price, or under what terms.

Some systems publish call-offs above a threshold. Others publish only the framework award. Some publish a summary in a contract register. The result is a patchwork that makes comparison difficult. A researcher can see that a supplier is on a framework but not how much work it received.

The call-off also determines competition. A framework may allow a mini-competition among participants, or it may allow a direct award to one supplier. Those are different competition models. A framework that looks competitive at the top may produce single-supplier orders at the bottom.

The subcontracting blind spot

Subcontracting is where the public record often ends. A prime contractor may be accountable for delivery, but much of the work may be performed by subcontractors. The prime may add management, integration, and risk transfer. That can be valuable. It can also add a layer of cost without a corresponding layer of visibility.

Subcontracting plans are often filed at the framework or prime-contract level. They may state a percentage or a category of work, but not the actual subcontracts. The public may know that a small business is involved without knowing which one, for how much, or on what terms.

The blind spot matters for accountability and for market access. If a framework is supposed to open opportunities to smaller firms, the public should be able to see whether those opportunities materialized. If a prime is supposed to add value, the public should be able to see what it actually did.

SME access and the lotting decision

Framework design affects who can bid. A single national framework for a broad category may be accessible only to large firms with the capacity to cover every region and service. A lot structure can open the same category to specialists, regional firms, and smaller suppliers.

Lotting is not a cost-free choice. More lots mean more evaluation, more contract management, and more coordination. A buyer may prefer a single prime for simplicity. That preference can be legitimate, but it should be weighed against the loss of competition and the risk of dependency.

The public record should show the lot structure, the number of bidders per lot, and the outcome. A framework that reports only the total number of suppliers can hide the fact that one lot attracted no competition or that one supplier won most of the work.

How to read a framework price

A framework price is often a rate card, not a final price. The buyer may negotiate a discount, a maximum rate, or a pricing mechanism for call-offs. The actual price depends on the order, the volume, and the competition at call-off stage.

A reader should therefore compare like with like. A framework rate for a generic labor category is not comparable to a fixed-price call-off for a defined deliverable. A discount off a list price is not comparable to a competitively tendered price.

The contract record should show the pricing basis, the call-off value, and any amendments. Without that chain, a framework can look cheap at the top and expensive at the bottom, or vice versa, without anyone being able to prove it.

What auditors look for

Audit institutions tend to focus on whether the framework delivered value, whether call-offs were competed, and whether the buyer managed performance. They also look at whether the framework was used for work outside its scope, a practice sometimes called call-off creep.

The audit trail should show the requirement, the route to market, the competition, the award, and the performance. If any of those steps is missing, the auditor has to rely on management explanations. That is weaker evidence than a contemporaneous record.

Auditors also look at concentration. If a small number of suppliers win most of the call-offs, the framework may be less competitive than it appears. Concentration is not automatically a problem, but it is a pattern that should be explained.

The data model that makes frameworks legible

A framework can be made legible with a small number of data fields: the framework identifier, the lot, the call-off identifier, the buyer, the supplier, the value, the procedure used, and the subcontracting threshold. Those fields allow a reader to connect the vehicle to the order and the order to the supply chain.

The fields should use stable identifiers, not free text. A supplier name is not enough if the same group operates under several legal entities. A lot description is not enough if it changes between the framework and the call-off. Identifiers are what make aggregation possible.

Publication should be timely. A call-off published two years after the work is finished is better than nothing, but it is not useful for live accountability. A quarterly release, in a machine-readable format, would let researchers and oversight bodies track the framework as it is used.

The data model also needs a rule for amendments. A call-off can change in value, scope, or duration. If the amendment is not linked to the original, the public sees a new number without a baseline. A simple version history would solve most of that problem.

None of this requires a new mega-system. It requires a consistent schema and a publication habit. The technology is the easy part. The difficult part is persuading buyers that the call-off and the subcontract are part of the public record.

Designing a framework that can be checked

First, publish the call-off data in a standard format. Second, require a subcontracting map at call-off level, not only at framework level. Third, use lots where the market is diverse. Fourth, compete call-offs where the framework allows it. Fifth, report concentration and bidder numbers by lot.

Sixth, keep the framework under review. A framework that was competitive at launch may become stale after several years. A periodic market test, or a new competition, keeps the pressure on.

The final lesson is that frameworks are not inherently opaque. They become opaque when the buyer treats the framework award as the end of the procurement story. The call-off and the subcontract are where the public money is actually committed. They should be where the transparency begins.

A framework that publishes its call-offs, its subcontracts, and its concentration data is not a burden on suppliers. It is a signal that the buyer intends to manage the vehicle, not just launch it. That signal is worth more than another round of promotional material about how many suppliers are on the framework.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. A framework is a permission to buy; the call-off is the real contract.
  2. Call-off transparency is essential for understanding price, scope, and competition.
  3. Subcontracting plans are not a substitute for subcontract-level data.
  4. Lot design determines whether smaller suppliers can realistically compete.
  5. Concentration and bidder numbers should be reported by lot, not just in total.
