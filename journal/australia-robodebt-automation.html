Robodebt: Automation Without an Evidence Base

Australia's Robodebt scheme used income averaging to raise debts at scale. The royal commission found it unlawful and unfair. The procurement lesson is about buying automation before the policy and the evidence are ready.

What the scheme did

Robodebt was an Australian programme that sought to recover welfare overpayments by comparing reported income with data from the tax authority. Where the data suggested a discrepancy, the system used income averaging to calculate a debt. The approach allowed the government to raise many debts quickly.

The problem was that income averaging is not the same as proof of an overpayment. A person's income may vary across the year, and the tax data may not match the period used by the welfare system. The method could produce a debt that the person did not actually owe.

The scheme was therefore an automation of a policy assumption, not an automation of a verified fact. That distinction is central. A system can be technically correct and still produce unlawful outcomes if the rule it applies is not supported by the law.

The legal advice problem

The royal commission examined what the government knew about the legal basis for the scheme and when it knew it. The finding was damning: the scheme was unlawful, and the risks were not adequately addressed. Legal advice is not a procurement document, but it is part of the evidence a buyer needs before buying automation.

A procurement process can require a legal opinion, but it cannot guarantee that the opinion is correct or that it is acted on. The deeper issue is governance. Who has the authority to stop a programme when the legal basis is uncertain? Who is accountable when the policy and the system disagree?

The case also shows the danger of treating a legal review as a one-time gate. A scheme can change over time, and a legal opinion given at the start may not cover later uses of data or later changes to the calculation. The review has to follow the system.

The human in the loop

Robodebt was often described as automated, but people were involved. Officials reviewed some cases, and the system relied on administrative processes. The question is whether those people had the information and authority to challenge the automated result.

If an official can only confirm a system-generated debt, the human is a rubber stamp. If an official can see the underlying data and the legal rule, the human can provide real oversight. The difference is not a job title. It is the design of the workflow.

Procurement should specify that workflow. It should require an explanation of the calculation, access to the source data, a way to record a challenge, and a process for correcting an error. Those features are not optional extras. They are the safeguards that make automation accountable.

Data matching is not proof

Data matching can be a useful tool for detecting errors, but a match is not a debt. It is a signal that two records may be inconsistent. The inconsistency can be caused by timing, a data-entry error, a different definition of income, or a genuine overpayment. Each cause requires a different response.

The procurement requirement should reflect that uncertainty. A system that treats a match as a debt has embedded a legal conclusion in a data process. A system that flags a match for review preserves the distinction between suspicion and proof.

The quality of the data also matters. If the buyer does not control the source data, it may not know how accurate it is or how it was collected. A contract that requires data matching without a data-quality clause is a contract that transfers an unknown risk to the public.

The cost of getting it wrong

The financial cost of Robodebt included the settlement of a class action, the cost of refunds and administration, and the cost of the royal commission. The social cost included stress, debt, and loss of trust in the welfare system. Those costs are not in the original business case.

A procurement business case should include the cost of error, not just the cost of the system. If an automated decision can cause financial harm, the buyer needs a correction process, an appeal process, and a way to estimate the rate of false positives. Those requirements should be funded.

The case also shows that savings claims can be self-fulfilling. If a programme counts every raised debt as a saving, it may not distinguish between a legitimate recovery and an unlawful charge. The metric has to be tied to the legal outcome, not to the volume of notices.

The advice chain is part of the system

Large public IT programmes rely on external advice: legal opinions, technical reviews, actuarial analysis, and programme assurance. That advice can improve decisions, but it can also create a false sense of confidence. A report that says a scheme is feasible is not the same as a legal opinion that it is lawful.

The procurement record should show who provided which advice, what they were asked, and what they concluded. If the advice was ignored, the record should show why. If the advice was not sought, that absence is itself a governance finding.

The Robodebt case is a reminder that advisers are not decision-makers. The buyer remains accountable. A contract that buys advice without buying the capability to challenge it is a contract that may simply purchase confirmation.

Metrics that do not reward harm

Programme metrics shape behaviour. If a team is measured by the number of debts raised, it will raise more debts. If it is measured by the value recovered, it may pursue large cases and ignore errors. A balanced scorecard should include accuracy, appeal outcomes, and the cost of corrections.

The metric should also distinguish between a debt raised and a debt confirmed. A raised debt is a claim. A confirmed debt has survived review or appeal. Reporting the first as if it were the second inflates the programme's apparent success.

Those metrics need to be in the contract, not only in an annual report. A supplier that is paid for volume will optimise for volume. A supplier that is paid for verified outcomes will optimise for verification. The incentive design is part of the procurement.

Metrics also need a baseline. A fall in appeals may mean better decisions, or it may mean that people have given up. A rise in recoveries may mean better detection, or it may mean more false positives. Without a control group or an independent review, a single trend can be read in opposite ways.

The publication of those metrics is part of accountability. If the programme reports only its successes, the public cannot see the error rate. A small, regular release of accuracy data, appeal outcomes, and correction costs would make the programme easier to judge and harder to misrepresent.

That release should be designed before the system goes live, because retrofitting a measurement framework is slow and often incomplete. The contract should require the data fields that the metrics need, not just the reports that management wants.

What to require before automating a decision

First, prove the legal basis for the rule the system will apply. Second, prove that the data supports the inference the system will make. Third, design human review with access to evidence and authority to change the result. Fourth, measure false positives, not just recoveries. Fifth, preserve an explanation for every decision.

Sixth, build a stop mechanism. If the legal basis changes or the error rate is too high, the buyer must be able to pause the system without waiting for a new procurement. That requires contract terms, technical capability, and a governance process.

The final lesson is about the difference between efficiency and lawfulness. A system that raises more debts faster is not necessarily a better system. If the debts are not owed, the speed is simply a faster way to cause harm.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. Automating a policy assumption is not the same as automating a verified fact.
  2. A data match is a signal for review, not proof of a debt.
  3. Human oversight requires access to evidence and authority to change the result.
  4. Business cases should include the cost of false positives and corrections.
  5. A stop mechanism is a core requirement for high-volume automated decisions.
