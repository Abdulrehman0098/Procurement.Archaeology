Defence Offsets: Industrial Policy Inside the Contract

Offsets promise jobs, technology, and exports in exchange for a defence purchase. They also make the true price of the deal harder to see. The public question is whether the industrial benefits are real, additional, and worth the cost.

What an offset is

A defence offset is an obligation attached to a defence purchase. The supplier agrees to provide industrial work, technology transfer, investment, or export support in the buyer's country. In return, the buyer may count those benefits against the contract's value.

Offsets can be direct or indirect. A direct offset involves work on the purchased system, such as local production or maintenance. An indirect offset involves activity in another sector, such as civil investment or export promotion. Both are intended to create industrial benefits beyond the immediate purchase.

The policy rationale is usually a mix of security, jobs, and technology. A government may want to reduce dependence on foreign suppliers, sustain a domestic defence industry, or secure access to critical technology. Those are legitimate objectives, but they have a cost.

The price is not just the contract value

An offset can raise the price of a defence purchase. The supplier may charge more to cover the cost of local production, technology transfer, or guaranteed work. It may also accept a lower margin in exchange for a larger order. The net effect is hard to see without the offset agreement.

The public should be able to compare the offset-adjusted price with the price of a comparable purchase without offsets. That comparison is rarely available. The offset is often negotiated as part of a package, and the counterfactual is not published.

The value of the offset also depends on the multiplier. If a country credits a dollar of investment as two dollars of offset, the obligation can be met with less actual activity. Multipliers are a policy choice, but they should be disclosed and justified.

Additionality is the hard question

The key test is additionality: would the industrial activity have happened without the offset? If a supplier would have invested in the country anyway, the offset may be a label on existing activity rather than a new benefit.

Additionality is difficult to prove. It requires a counterfactual, which is not observable. A government can use benchmarks, independent studies, and audits, but the conclusion will always involve judgment. The public record should show the method.

A related test is durability. An offset that creates a factory which closes after the contract ends may not create a lasting capability. An offset that transfers technology without the skills to use it may not create a real capability either. The evaluation should look beyond the contract period.

Trade rules and security exceptions

Offsets are controversial in trade policy. The WTO Government Procurement Agreement generally discourages offsets in covered procurement, but defence procurement is often excluded or subject to security exceptions. The result is a patchwork of national rules.

In the EU, defence procurement is governed by a specific directive and by national security considerations. The rules restrict offsets in some contexts while leaving space for industrial policy in others. The details matter, and they vary by member state.

For a researcher, the legal framework is the first stop. An offset that is lawful in one country may be restricted in another. A claim that an offset is required by security should be tested against the specific legal basis, not accepted as a slogan.

What auditors and parliaments can see

Offsets are often less transparent than the underlying defence contract. The contract may be classified, and the offset agreement may be confidential. A parliament may receive a summary, but not the full terms. That makes independent evaluation difficult.

Audit institutions can examine whether the offset obligations were met, whether the credits were valued correctly, and whether the claimed benefits materialized. They can also ask whether the offset was cost-effective compared with other ways of supporting the industrial base.

The public record should include the offset obligation, the credit methodology, the annual performance, and the evaluation. Without those, the public cannot tell whether the offset is a real industrial strategy or a accounting device.

Are offsets the best tool?

A government that wants to support a defence industrial base has other tools: direct investment, research and development funding, export credit, and long-term procurement commitments. An offset is one instrument among many, and it may not be the most efficient.

An offset can be useful when it leverages a large purchase to create a capability that would not otherwise exist. It is less useful when it simply relabels activity that the market would have delivered. The choice should be based on a comparison of instruments, not on a preference for offsets as a condition of sale.

The comparison should include the cost of the offset, the risk of dependency, and the opportunity cost of the industrial policy. A job created by an offset is not free if the offset raises the price of the equipment or distorts the market.

Technology transfer is not the same as capability

A technology-transfer obligation can look impressive on paper. The supplier agrees to share designs, processes, or source code. The real question is whether the recipient can use the transfer to build, maintain, or improve the system without the supplier.

Capability requires skills, tools, quality systems, and a market. A design package without trained engineers is not a capability. A factory without a supply chain is not a capability. An offset that stops at documentation may satisfy the contract but not the policy goal.

The buyer should therefore define the capability outcome, not just the transfer activity. Can the local firm maintain the system? Can it produce a defined component? Can it export? Those outcomes are measurable in a way that a document count is not.

The supplier's incentive matters too. A supplier may prefer a transfer that does not create a future competitor. The contract should specify what is transferred, to whom, and under what rights. Without that specificity, the offset can become a training programme rather than an industrial strategy.

Intellectual property is the crux. A licence to use a design is not the same as ownership of it. A right to maintain a system is not the same as a right to modify it. The contract should state which rights are needed for the capability, and what happens if the supplier exits the market.

The buyer should also consider the export dimension. A local capability that cannot export may be sustainable only while the domestic programme lasts. An offset that creates an export-capable firm may be more valuable, but it may also require the supplier to share markets or technology. Those trade-offs should be explicit.

How to read an offset deal

Start with the obligation: what is the supplier required to deliver, by when, and at what value? Then look at the credit methodology. Then look at additionality and durability. Then look at the cost premium. Then look at the oversight record.

Be sceptical of headline offset values. A large offset package can be less valuable than a smaller one if the credits are inflated or the activity would have happened anyway. The useful number is the net benefit, not the gross commitment.

The final lesson is about transparency. A defence offset is public money by another name. It should be subject to the same standards of evidence as the contract it accompanies. If it cannot be evaluated, it cannot be defended.

A government that publishes the obligation, the methodology, the annual performance, and the independent evaluation will take some political risk. But it will also build the evidence needed to defend a legitimate industrial policy. Secrecy may avoid a short-term argument, but it leaves the policy vulnerable to the next audit.

How this analysis was built

Procurement Archaeology works from the public record outward. For this analysis we began with the buyer's own contract notices, award notices, and amendment records, then compared them with audit reports, court filings, corporate registry data, and parliamentary or congressional oversight documents. We treated every figure as provisional until it could be tied to a dated document. Where a number appears in a headline but not in the underlying record, we say so rather than repeat it.

We also distinguish between the value of a contract, the amount actually paid, and the estimated whole-life cost; those are not interchangeable. Our source hierarchy places official journals and audit institutions first, court records second, and reputable reporting third. We link to the primary document whenever one is available. If a document is not public, we describe the gap instead of filling it with inference.

This article is explanatory analysis, not a legal finding. Anyone named here is presumed to have acted lawfully unless a court or regulator has found otherwise. Corrections are published with a dated note. The author is Abdulrehman Bhatti, Founder & Lead Researcher.

Readers can reproduce the method. Start with the cited source, identify the legal instrument, and separate the contract value from the payment and the whole-life cost. Then check the modification history and the subcontracting chain. Where the article uses a reported figure, ask whether the underlying document is public and whether the figure is an estimate, a ceiling, an obligation, or a payment. Where the record is incomplete, treat the gap as a finding rather than an invitation to speculate. That discipline is the difference between a public-record analysis and a confident opinion.

What this case teaches

  1. An offset is an industrial-policy obligation attached to a defence purchase, not a discount.
  2. The true price includes any premium the supplier charges to fulfil the offset.
  3. Additionality and durability are the central tests of whether an offset creates real value.
  4. Trade rules and security exceptions vary by jurisdiction and should be checked.
  5. Offsets should be compared with other industrial-policy instruments, not assumed to be necessary.
