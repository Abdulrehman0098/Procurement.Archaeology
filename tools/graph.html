Graph guide: tracing a supply chain

How to read a buyer-supplier-subcontractor graph without mistaking a line for proof.

Open the interactive view →

buyersuppliersubcontractorsource

The graph view shows how buyers, suppliers, and subcontractors can be arranged as a network. A line between two nodes is a relationship claim. The editorial question is whether that claim is supported by a contract, a corporate filing, a court record, or an audit. A graph is a map of hypotheses, not a verdict.

Data status: the interactive view uses a labelled demonstration dataset. This guide explains how to read the interface and how to verify a real record.

What the graph view shows

The graph view places buyers on one side, suppliers in the middle, and subcontractors on the outside. The visual language is simple: nodes are entities, lines are relationships, and colours distinguish roles. The demo uses synthetic records so that the interaction can be explored without exposing personal data or misrepresenting a real company.

In a real investigation, each line should have a source. A prime contract establishes a buyer-supplier link. A subcontract establishes a supplier-subcontractor link. A corporate filing can establish ownership. A court judgment can establish a dispute. Without a source, a line is only a possibility.

Reading the network

Start with the buyer. A buyer with many lines may be a large procuring entity; a buyer with one line may be a single-project agency. Then look at the supplier. A supplier with many lines across different buyers may be a repeat contractor; a supplier with one line may be a specialist. Then look at the subcontractors. A cluster of subcontractors with the same address, the same registration date, or the same director is a pattern that deserves a document check.

Do not read the graph as a hierarchy of blame. A subcontractor is not automatically a shell company. A prime contractor is not automatically a pass-through. The graph is useful because it makes relationships visible; it is dangerous when it makes them look proven.

From line to document

The strongest graph is built backwards. Start with a document, then add the entity and the relationship it proves. A contract notice proves a buyer-supplier relationship. An amendment proves a change in scope or value. A corporate registry extract proves a parent-subsidiary relationship. A court filing proves a dispute, not necessarily a wrongdoing.

When two sources conflict, keep both. A graph that hides conflict is less useful than a graph that shows it. The same supplier can be a subsidiary in one register and a separate legal entity in another; the discrepancy is part of the story.

Limits and common errors

Graphs are visually persuasive, which makes them easy to over-read. A thick line does not mean a large contract; it means the visualization chose a thick line. A central node does not mean control; it means the layout placed it there. A missing line does not mean no relationship; it means the dataset does not contain it.

Common errors include treating a shared address as proof of a shell company, treating a shared director as proof of coordination, and treating a subcontract as proof that the prime contractor added no value. Each of those claims requires a document and a legal framework.

Frequently asked questions

Is the graph based on real companies?

The interactive graph uses demonstration data. It is a visual demo, not a statement about any real company. The investigations use public records and cite them.

What does a line between two nodes mean?

In the demo, a line is a synthetic relationship. In a real investigation, a line should be backed by a contract, a corporate filing, a court record, or an audit.

Why are subcontractors shown separately?

Because subcontracting is often where the public record becomes thin. A prime contract may be published while the subcontracts are not. Showing the layer separately makes that gap visible.

Can a graph prove collusion?

No. Collusion is a legal finding that requires evidence and, usually, an enforcement decision. A graph can show patterns that merit investigation, but it cannot prove intent.

How do I check a company identity?

Use official corporate registers, LEI records, and beneficial-ownership registers. Compare registration numbers, addresses, and ownership chains. A name match alone is not enough.

What should I do if the graph is incomplete?

Treat the gap as a finding. Missing subcontracts, redacted values, and unidentified entities are part of the accountability story.
