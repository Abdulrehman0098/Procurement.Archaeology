Search guide: reading a contract record

How to use the search view to move from a supplier name to a contract, a buyer, an amendment, and a source document.

Open the interactive view →

The search view is the front door to the demonstration archive. It is designed to show how a structured procurement record can be filtered by country, sector, year, procedure, and number of bids. The editorial lesson is not the demo data itself. It is the sequence of questions a researcher should ask when a search result appears.

Data status: the interactive view uses a labelled demonstration dataset. This guide explains how to read the interface and how to verify a real record.

What the search view does

The search view takes a free-text query and combines it with five filters: country, sector, year, procedure, and bid count. It returns a table of contract records with the title, buyer, supplier, value, procedure, and number of bids. The table is a starting point, not an answer. A record that looks unusual in the demo may be perfectly ordinary in the real world, and a record that looks ordinary may hide a problem in an amendment or a subcontract.

Use the search view to practice a disciplined sequence: search broadly, narrow by buyer or supplier, then open the underlying record. Do not treat a single row as a finding. Treat it as a citation that still needs a source.

A five-step reading routine

Step one: identify the buyer. A buyer can be a ministry, an agency, a local authority, a hospital, or a state-owned company. The legal identity matters because the same name can cover different entities.

Step two: identify the supplier. Check whether the named supplier is the prime contractor, a subsidiary, a joint-venture member, or a trading name. A parent company may not be the contracting entity.

Step three: identify the instrument. An open procedure, a negotiated procedure, a restricted procedure, and a framework call-off have different competition rules and different transparency expectations.

Step four: identify the number. Is it an estimate, an award value, a ceiling, a payment, or a final cost? A contract value without a label is not a fact.

Step five: find the source. If the record cannot be tied to a notice, an award, an amendment, or an audit, keep looking.

What the filters reveal

Filters are analytical instruments. A country filter can expose a concentration of awards in one jurisdiction. A sector filter can show whether a supplier appears across health, defence, and transport. A year filter can reveal a surge in emergency awards. A procedure filter can separate open competition from negotiated awards. A bid-count filter can surface single-bidder contracts, which are a risk indicator rather than proof of wrongdoing.

The most useful filter is often the one you did not expect to use. If a supplier appears only in one country, one sector, and one procedure type, that pattern is worth checking against the source record. If a buyer appears with many small awards just below a threshold, that is also a pattern worth checking, but only after the legal thresholds and the buyer's own rules are understood.

Limits and common errors

The demo table is synthetic. It is not a live feed, and it does not contain the full universe of public contracts. Even a live system would have gaps: notices are amended, values are redacted, identifiers change, and some awards are never published in a machine-readable form. A search result is a lead, not a conclusion.

Common errors include treating a framework ceiling as money spent, treating a parent company as the contracting entity, and treating a single-bid award as evidence of corruption. Each of those errors can be avoided by reading the underlying document and labelling the number correctly.

Frequently asked questions

Does the search view use live procurement data?

No. The interactive table is a demonstration dataset. It is labelled as synthetic wherever it appears. The investigations on this site use public records and cite them separately.

What does a single-bid result mean?

A single-bid result means that one bid was recorded in the dataset. It is a risk indicator, not proof of wrongdoing. The procedure, the market, the specification, and the legal rules all matter.

Why do contract values look incomplete?

The demo is designed to show the interface, not to provide a financial ledger. In real research, a value must be tied to an award notice, an amendment, a payment record, or an audit. If that link is missing, the number is not a finding.

Can I export the results?

The demo includes a CSV export in the interactive search view. The export is a convenience for exploring the demonstration data, not a substitute for the source documents.

How do I verify a record I find?

Start with the buyer's procurement portal, then check the official journal for the jurisdiction. For EU awards, use TED. For the US, use SAM.gov and USAspending. For the UK, use Contracts Finder and Find a Tender.

Why does the guide focus on process rather than the demo numbers?

Because the process is transferable. The demo data will change; the discipline of identifying the buyer, supplier, instrument, number, and source does not.
