Map guide: putting a project in place

How geographic context changes the way a procurement record should be read.

Open the interactive view →

The map view places public projects in geographic context. Location is not decoration. It affects the market, the buyer, the supply chain, the environmental review, the labour rules, and the political accountability. A project that looks efficient in a table can look very different on a map.

Data status: the interactive view uses a labelled demonstration dataset. This guide explains how to read the interface and how to verify a real record.

What the map view shows

The map view groups projects by location and sector. The demo uses synthetic coordinates and records. The purpose is to show how a researcher can move from a contract row to a place, and from a place to a set of questions that are not visible in the row.

In a real investigation, a map can reveal concentration: many awards in one region, a cluster of projects near a border, or a supplier that appears repeatedly in one area. It can also reveal a mismatch between the stated beneficiary and the actual location of the work.

Why location matters

Procurement is legal and political at the same time. A national framework may be signed in a capital, but the work happens in a region with its own labour market, environmental rules, and local government. A hospital contract may be awarded by a regional health service, but the construction supply chain may be national or international.

Location also affects the evidence. A local audit office may publish a report that a national portal does not link to. A regional court may hear a dispute that never appears in the procurement journal. A map can help you find those local sources.

Reading a project pin

Start with the buyer and the site. Is the buyer the same entity that will use the asset? Is the site inside the buyer's jurisdiction? Is the project part of a larger programme? Then look at the supply chain. Are the suppliers local, national, or foreign? Does the contract require local subcontracting? If so, is there evidence that the requirement was met?

Then look at the timeline. A project that was announced, delayed, redesigned, and completed is a different story from a project that was announced and cancelled. The map should be read with the timeline, not instead of it.

Limits and common errors

Maps imply precision. A pin on a map can suggest that a project is exactly there, when the source only gives a city or a region. A cluster can suggest concentration, when the underlying data simply uses the same geocoding rule. A blank area can suggest no activity, when the real reason is missing data.

Common errors include treating a registered address as the place of performance, treating a project's political beneficiary as its legal buyer, and treating a map cluster as proof of favoritism. Each requires a document check.

Frequently asked questions

Are the map pins real projects?

The interactive map uses demonstration data. The pins are synthetic and are not statements about real projects. The investigations cite public records separately.

Why does location matter in procurement?

Location affects the market, the buyer's jurisdiction, labour and environmental rules, local audit coverage, and the political accountability for a project.

Can a map show corruption?

No. A map can show patterns that merit investigation, but corruption is a legal finding that requires evidence and, usually, an enforcement decision.

What if a project has no precise location?

Use the most specific level the source supports, such as a region or city, and say so. Do not invent a pin for visual convenience.

How do I find local sources?

Check the buyer's own website, regional audit offices, local courts, municipal council minutes, and regional procurement portals. National journals often link to them.

Does the map replace the contract record?

No. The map is a way to ask better questions. The contract record remains the primary evidence.
