Methodology

A reproducible account of how Procurement Archaeology finds, verifies, and presents evidence from public procurement records.

1. Principles

The method is built around four rules. First, start with a document, not a claim. Second, distinguish what the record says from what an actor says about the record. Third, preserve uncertainty instead of hiding it. Fourth, make the trail reproducible so a reader can check the work.

These rules matter because procurement is a documentary system. A contract notice is not the same as an award notice. An award notice is not the same as an amendment. An amendment is not the same as a payment. A payment is not the same as a final cost. Each step can change the meaning of the story.

2. Source hierarchy

Primary sources are official procurement journals and buyer records: TED in the EU, SAM.gov and USAspending in the United States, Contracts Finder and Find a Tender in the United Kingdom, national portals, court judgments, and audit reports from institutions such as the NAO, GAO, European Court of Auditors, and national audit offices.

Secondary sources are corporate registries, beneficial-ownership registers, LEI records, customs data, parliamentary questions, committee reports, and regulatory decisions. These help identify relationships and inconsistencies, but they do not replace the procurement document.

Tertiary sources are reputable reporting, NGO research, and specialist trade publications. They are used to locate a document or to understand context, not to establish a contested fact on their own.

3. Verification

Every factual claim should be traceable to at least one dated document. A contested or negative claim should have corroboration from a second independent source or an official finding. Where that is not possible, the article uses qualified language and says what is missing.

Confidence is described in plain language rather than a false numerical score. Documented means the primary record says it. Corroborated means more than one independent source supports it. Disputed means the sources conflict. Unresolved means the record is incomplete and the article does not pretend otherwise.

4. Handling money

Contract values are not all the same. A tender estimate, an award value, a ceiling, a call-off value, an amendment, a payment, and a whole-life cost are different numbers. Articles label which one is being discussed. Currency, tax treatment, price base, and exchange-rate date are stated where they matter.

When a figure is reported in the press but cannot be tied to a primary document, the article says so. When a value is redacted, the article describes the redaction rather than estimating a substitute.

5. Entity resolution

Company names are not unique. A subsidiary, a joint venture, a trading name, and a parent company can appear as different entities in different registers. The method uses registration numbers, LEI codes, addresses, and ownership records to distinguish them. If the link is probable but not proven, the article says “probable” and explains why.

6. Right of reply and corrections

Before publishing a negative finding about an identifiable company or person, we make a reasonable effort to contact them and reflect a substantive response. A response is published alongside the finding, or the article explains why no response was received.

Corrections are published with a dated note. A correction is not a silent edit. If a figure changes, the article says what changed and why.

7. Automation and AI

Software can help find documents, extract fields, deduplicate entities, and flag anomalies. It cannot decide what is true. Automated output is treated as a lead, not a finding. Every published claim is reviewed against the underlying document by a human editor.

The interactive demo on the home page uses synthetic data to demonstrate search, graph, map, and archive interfaces. It is not a live ingestion system and it is not used as evidence in the investigations.

8. Limits

Public procurement records are incomplete by design. Commercial confidentiality, redaction, poor portal design, and inconsistent identifiers all create gaps. The method does not fill those gaps with speculation. It documents them.
